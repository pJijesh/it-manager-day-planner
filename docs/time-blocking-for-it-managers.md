# Time blocking for IT managers

Practitioner guidance, not research. These are habits that tend to work for people running small and mid-size IT functions, written as suggestions to try and adapt. Where the planner makes a choice (a 30 percent buffer, a 30 minute minimum block), that is a starting value to tune, not a finding.

An IT manager's day is split between planned work and demand that arrives uninvited: incidents, urgent requests, questions from the team, executives who need an answer now. A plan that ignores the second kind is wrong by 10:00. A plan that is only the second kind is how a manager ends the week having done no managing. Time blocking is a way of making room for both on purpose.

## 1. Plan for interruptions instead of pretending they will not happen

- Reserve a share of the day for interruptions and incidents and put it in the diary as blocks, not as a feeling. The planner defaults to 30 percent. If you keep a rough tally for a fortnight of how much time unplanned work really took, set the figure from your own numbers.
- Spread the reserve through the day in blocks of about an hour. One big block at the end of the day is a catch-up slot; several smaller ones let you absorb a problem at 10:30 without losing the afternoon.
- When nothing interrupts you, the blocks are not wasted: use them for the backlog of small tasks, replies and people. Treat unplanned free time as a gift, not as proof the buffer was too big.
- Raise the buffer on days you can predict will be hard: a change window, a go-live, a month end, the day after a major incident. `--buffer 50` exists for this.

## 2. Protect deep work

- Pick a window when you do your hardest thinking (a proposal, a design, a difficult email, a budget narrative) and defend it. Many people choose the first part of the morning, but the right time is whichever you do your best work in.
- Put the deep-work window in the profile and mark the work `deep`. The planner keeps other tasks out of it until the deep tasks have been placed.
- Do not fill the window with routine checks. Do the alerts and queue check before it, and give the window to one task.
- Turn off notifications for the block, tell the team when you are back, and agree a way to reach you for a real emergency.
- If meetings keep landing in the window, the planner will warn you. Move the meeting, or move the window, and accept that some days there will be none.

## 3. Batch low-value work

- Group small, similar tasks (approvals, replies, expenses, access requests) into one block and do them in a row. Switching between unlike tasks tends to cost attention, so grouping similar ones usually feels lighter.
- Put the batch in a spot with low energy, such as just after lunch, and give it a fixed length. When time is up, stop.
- Decide a few things once so they stop costing you decisions: which requests you approve without a meeting, which you delegate and the spending level at which you want to see them.
- Handle email and chat at set times, not continuously. A fast reply is rarely worth more than the focus it costs.

## 4. Incident days

- When a major incident starts, the day plan is void. Say so, and stop trying to follow it.
- The first job is to decide who is leading the incident. If it is you, hand over your other commitments to someone for the day. If it is someone else, resist the urge to hover: be available to remove obstacles and to talk to the business.
- Keep one running note of times, facts and decisions (the [incident handover note](../templates/incident-handover-note.md) is a good shape). It will save you at the next shift change and in the review.
- Decide the next update time and keep to it, even if the update is "no change". Silence tends to bring more chasing than bad news does.
- At the end of the day, run the [shutdown checklist](../templates/end-of-day-shutdown.md), move what slipped on purpose, and set a short review for later in the week: what happened, what we changed.
- Tell people what you have dropped. "I have moved the budget review to Thursday because of the outage" is a message people accept.

## 5. How to say no and renegotiate

Most plans fail through yes, not through bad scheduling. The planner's Not today list is there so that a "no" or "not yet" is visible and honest.

- **Show the trade-off, not the refusal.** "I can do that today if the licence review moves to next week. Which matters more?" This puts the choice with the person who owns the priorities.
- **Offer a date.** "I cannot do it today. I can have it for you by Thursday at noon." A clear date is usually better received than a vague yes.
- **Ask what the deadline is for.** Many urgent requests turn out to be due next week.
- **Offer less.** A one-page answer by Tuesday, or a rough estimate now and a firm one later.
- **Delegate with context.** Pass the task and the outcome you want, and say who has the authority to decide.
- **Say it early.** A renegotiation on Monday is a conversation. The same message on the due date is an apology.
- **Keep a record.** Note what you declined or moved and why in the task list. It helps in conversations about capacity.

When a manager above you adds work, ask for the priority order rather than refusing: "These are the five things I am doing this week. Where does the new one go?"

## 6. Delegation

- Delegate outcomes, not just tasks, and be clear about what good looks like, the deadline and how much authority comes with it.
- Delegate the work you are good at once it is routine, not only the work you dislike. Your time is for what only you can do.
- Check in at planned points rather than hovering. Put those check-ins into your own plan or they will not happen.
- Accept a different method if the result is right. If the standard matters, write it down so it can be met.
- Watch for the person who is always given the urgent work. Spread incident duties and cover so that knowledge does not sit in one head.

## 7. Handling a backlog

A long list is not a plan. When the backlog is large:

1. **Admit what it is.** Count the tasks and add up the estimates. Compare the total with the task capacity in your week plan. A list of 80 hours against nine hours of task capacity a week is a message to leadership, not a time-management problem.
2. **Delete.** Close anything stale, duplicated or no longer wanted. Most backlogs contain items that nobody will miss.
3. **Rate importance honestly.** If everything is a 5, nothing is. Aim for few 5s.
4. **Slice the large items.** A 12-hour task is hard to plan and easy to avoid. Split it into blocks of 60 to 120 minutes with a clear result for each.
5. **Fix the blocked ones.** Items waiting on someone else need an owner, a chase date and a question. The planner lists them under Excluded so that they do not disappear.
6. **Run a weekly tidy.** Fifteen to thirty minutes of pruning keeps the list usable.
7. **Say the number out loud.** "We have 60 hours of requests and about 10 hours of capacity a week. Here is how I propose we order them" is an honest conversation.

## 8. When the plan collapses

Some days, nothing goes to plan. When that happens:

1. **Stop and look.** One minute. What is on fire? What has a deadline today that cannot move?
2. **Pick the minimum.** Choose the two or three things that must happen today. Everything else is moved, on purpose.
3. **Tell people.** Send short messages to anyone whose work you are moving, with a new date. People forgive a slip they hear about early.
4. **Protect the basics.** Alerts and backups, the queue and the people check-ins still happen, even if shortened.
5. **Do the shutdown.** Ten minutes at the end of the day to update the list, hand over open incidents and choose tomorrow's first task.
6. **Do not punish yourself.** A day that collapsed is information: was the buffer too small, were there too many meetings, was the estimate wrong? Adjust one thing.
7. **Rebuild tomorrow's plan from the real list.** Run `day-planner plan` for the next day and let the new scores decide.

## 9. A short routine that ties it together

- **Friday afternoon:** weekly review (see the [template](../templates/weekly-review.md)), move or drop what slipped, book next week's large blocks.
- **Monday morning:** run the week view, look at the flags, send the messages that renegotiation needs.
- **Each morning:** run the day plan, check it fits, pick the top three.
- **Each evening:** shutdown checklist, tomorrow's first task.

The [operating rhythm](operating-rhythm.md) lists the recurring checks that belong in that routine, and [how the planner works](how-the-planner-works.md) explains every rule behind the plans.
